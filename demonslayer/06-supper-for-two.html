Book One · Act II · Chapter 6

Supper for Two

8 minute read · 1,425 words

Mio chose which way to go.

It took her a long time.

At the edge of the forest, the path divided around a cedar. Both branches were visible now, pale earth under scattered needles. Rengoku waited with the lantern while she looked from one to the other.

“You can come to the inn,” he said.

“I know.”

“Or we can walk a little further with you.”

She touched the patch on her sleeve.

“I would like to choose without anyone helping.”

Rengoku closed his mouth.

Beside him, Akaza studied a completely unremarkable tree.

At last, Mio took the right-hand path.

She did not thank them again. She had already done it once, quietly, on the veranda, and Rengoku was glad she seemed to consider that enough.

When she disappeared, he lifted the lantern.

“We should wait until—”

“She chose.”

Akaza was still looking at the tree.

Rengoku looked at the empty path.

Then he turned towards the inn.

They walked for several minutes before either spoke.

“You could have said that more pleasantly,” Rengoku observed.

“Would you have listened more pleasantly?”

“Probably not.”

Akaza made a short sound through his nose. It might have been pain. Rengoku decided not to investigate.

Nao opened the door before they reached it.

She looked at the tear in Rengoku's sleeve, the blood on Akaza's vest and the parcel containing four rice balls.

“An eventful walk.”

“We found the house,” Rengoku said.

“I can see you brought some of it back.”

Akaza looked down. A splinter the length of a chopstick protruded from the hem of his trousers.

He pulled it out.

“Do not put that on my veranda.”

He held it helplessly until she pointed towards the wood basket.

Inside, Nao cleaned their wounds with water that stung considerably more than Rengoku felt necessary. The cut on his upper arm was deep but narrow. His ribs needed a fresh wrap.

Akaza sat facing away while she examined his back.

Rengoku counted the cuts before he could stop himself.

Five long ones. Two shorter. A bruise spreading over the injured shoulder.

The memory of a fist passing through his chest arrived without warning.

He looked down at his own bandage.

Nao's hands kept moving.

Nothing had become simple in the forest.

When she finished, she set two bowls of soup on the table.

Rengoku waited for Akaza to sit before reaching for his chopsticks.

Akaza noticed. Neither mentioned it.

They ate in silence.

For Rengoku, it was a substantial effort.

“The soup is excellent,” he said eventually.

“I had gathered.”

“I had not said so.”

“You looked at it.”

Rengoku considered whether enthusiasm could be visible at a distance of three feet.

“Then it must be exceptionally excellent.”

Akaza took another mouthful.

The corner of his mouth moved.

Rengoku decided that this, too, did not require investigation.

Outside, the gorge roared through the place where the bridge ought to have been.

“Daichi will be all right for tonight,” Rengoku said.

Akaza nodded.

They had left the carpenter on the veranda of the house, with a blanket recovered from an unbroken cupboard. He had chosen the spot himself. Rui had carried a cup of water out and placed it on the floor several paces away.

Daichi had watched him return indoors before picking it up.

“We should check tomorrow,” Rengoku said.

“He said we could.”

“Yes.”

That mattered. Rengoku was beginning to notice how often it mattered.

Akaza turned his bowl a little, keeping his injured hand out of the steam.

“You saw the opening.”

Rengoku's chopsticks stopped.

“At Rui's neck,” Akaza said.

“Yes.”

“You would have reached him.”

Rengoku looked across the table.

“What would that have done to Mio?”

“I don't know.”

“Neither did I.”

He resumed eating.

Akaza did not.

After a while, he said, “I would once have called that hesitation.”

Rengoku swallowed.

“You would once have called many things weakness.”

The room changed around the words.

Akaza lowered his eyes.

“Yes.”

Rengoku set his chopsticks down. He had not meant the sentence as a test. He disliked how closely he had watched the answer.

“Your warning helped me see her wrist,” he said.

Akaza looked up.

“That is all I am saying.”

“I heard you.”

The silence returned. This time, both could eat inside it.

Nao brought more rice.

Rengoku thanked her with enough conviction to make the paper screens vibrate.

“Do you always do that?” Akaza asked when she had gone.

“Appreciate a good meal? Certainly.”

“At that volume.”

“Not always.”

Akaza looked at him.

“Sometimes I am louder.”

The sound that escaped Akaza was unmistakably a laugh. Small and rough, interrupted almost immediately when his shoulder hurt.

Rengoku smiled.

Then a remembered laugh rose beneath it, bright with excitement, on ground beside a broken train.

His smile faltered.

Akaza saw.

He did not ask what was wrong. He did not apologise for laughing or try to earn the smile back.

He waited.

Rengoku took a sip of tea.

“The passengers,” Akaza said after a while. “On the train.”

The cup stopped halfway to the table.

“What about them?”

“How many were there?”

“More than two hundred.”

Akaza looked towards the empty places in the room.

“Did you know any of them?”

“Not before that night.”

Rengoku remembered a woman's hand around a child's shoulder. A conductor's cap. Someone gripping a little cloth purse even after they were safely out.

“One man was very concerned about his lunch.”

Akaza blinked.

“His lunch?”

“The carriage had overturned. He had a cut over one eye. But his first question was whether anyone had seen the box.”

“Did you?”

“No.”

Rengoku glanced at the rice on the table.

“I shared mine.”

“Of course you did.”

The answer came too easily. Too familiar.

Akaza realised it at the same moment Rengoku did.

He looked down.

Rengoku could have let the conversation end there.

He nearly did.

Instead, he said, “There were children too. An elderly couple. People who had work the next day.”

He watched Akaza listen.

“I did not protect them because they were exceptional.”

Akaza's good hand rested open beside his bowl.

“You thought they would get to go home,” he said.

“I intended that they would.”

The river filled the pause between them.

“I never asked you about them,” Akaza said.

“No.”

“I only saw you.”

Rengoku looked at the man across the table, the blue marks, the bandage, the blood Nao had not quite been able to wash from the edge of his collar.

“They were there.”

“I know.”

“You knew then.”

Akaza closed his eyes briefly.

“Yes.”

He did not add anything.

Rengoku found he could accept the silence after that answer more easily than he could have accepted an explanation.

He finished his tea.

Later, they took the lantern out to the broken bridge post.

Rengoku held the light while Akaza examined the damage without trying to lift anything. Nao had been very clear about that.

“New anchors,” Akaza said. “The stone is split below the surface.”

“I know where we can get timber.”

“And rope?”

“Nao has a storeroom.”

Akaza glanced at him.

“We should ask before deciding what is in it.”

“A fair precaution!”

The thread around the lantern handle had become slack after Rui let Mio go. Now Rengoku unwound it carefully, one loop at a time. It came free without resistance.

He laid it on the post.

“Will it hold?” Akaza asked.

“The bridge?”

“What he did. Letting her go.”

Rengoku looked towards the trees.

“I don't know.”

Akaza nodded, as if the answer was more useful than reassurance.

“We will check on Daichi,” Rengoku said. “And we will keep our agreement.”

He picked up the lantern.

“That includes telling me when you cannot use your arm.”

Akaza looked at his shoulder.

“I can use it.”

Rengoku waited.

“Badly,” Akaza added.

“A valuable improvement in the report.”

They went back inside.

At the foot of the stairs, Rengoku paused. Above him, warm light lay beneath a cedar door.

He was tired. His ribs hurt. There were clean bandages on the table and someone else who would hear a traveller arrive.

His hand moved towards the stair rail.

Then he stopped.

“If someone comes,” he said, “fetch Nao. Do not go down to the broken crossing alone.”

Akaza nodded.

Rengoku climbed three steps, then looked back.

Akaza had moved his chair so that he could see the road through the open door.

Rengoku stood there for another moment.

Then he went upstairs.

End of Act II
