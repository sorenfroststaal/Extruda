Book One · Act I · Chapter 2

The Fight That Changes Nothing

7 minute read · 1,292 words

The traveller's name was Toma.

Rengoku learned this six planks from the far bank, when the man tried to apologise for being in the way.

“You are exactly where someone who needs help should be,” Rengoku said. “Now, Toma. Give me the bundle.”

Toma tightened his arms around it.

“I made them.”

“Then we shall be careful with them.”

The little wooden horse lay beside Akaza's foot. Rengoku watched him crouch, pick it up and place it on the bridge post. Slowly. As though approaching a dangerous animal.

Rengoku disliked understanding that the dangerous animal was him.

“Wait here,” he told Toma. “I am going to make sure the bank is safe.”

He stepped off the bridge and hung the lantern on the post beside the horse.

Akaza stood several paces away, his hands open.

“You remember me,” he said.

Rengoku stared at him.

“Yes.”

The single word seemed to strike harder than a shout.

“I did not know if—”

“If I would remember dying?”

Akaza lowered his eyes.

“Look at me.”

He did.

The anger had been manageable across the gorge. It had possessed a shape: a distant figure, a bridge between them, a task to perform first. Here, close enough to see the familiar blue marks, it spread through Rengoku's chest until breathing itself felt like giving ground.

“The boys,” Rengoku said. “Did you go back?”

“No.”

“Did they live?”

“They lived beyond that morning.”

“That is not the same answer.”

“It is the answer I have.”

Akaza swallowed.

“Muzan is gone.”

The words landed strangely. For an instant, Rengoku saw a room full of faces, some he knew and some he had never met, listening to news they had paid for without living to hear.

“Who?” he asked.

Akaza understood.

“I do not know everyone who fell.”

“But you know some.”

“Yes.”

“And you came here.”

“I did not choose the road.”

“You chose plenty before it.”

Akaza flinched. Rengoku saw it and hated that part of him was satisfied.

Wind caught the white edge of his haori. Akaza's gaze followed it, then dropped to the place beneath Rengoku's ribs.

Rengoku drew his sword.

Akaza moved on instinct.

His heel ground into the earth. Blue light flashed beneath him in the outline of a snowflake. Rengoku recognised the stance before he consciously recognised the threat.

“Flame Breathing. First Form.”

His blade left a burning arc across the dusk.

“Unknowing Fire!”

Akaza twisted. Steel opened a red line from his shoulder to his chest. Blood struck the stones. His answering fist stopped a finger's width from Rengoku's jaw.

Stopped.

Rengoku drove his elbow into Akaza's throat and sent him crashing backwards through a low stone marker.

“Do not,” he said, “offer me that.”

Akaza rose coughing. Blood ran over the blue lines on his neck.

“What?”

“A fight you intend to lose.”

Something shifted in Akaza's face. Shame, briefly naked; then the old hard set of his mouth.

“You drew first.”

“And you decided what it meant before I had swung.”

Akaza's fist struck the flat of the next cut with a crack that numbed Rengoku's fingers. This time he followed through. A kick slammed into Rengoku's side and lifted him off the ground.

He landed on one knee. Pain burst along his ribs.

For one terrible heartbeat, he could not get air.

The ground was cold.

Not again.

He pulled breath into his lungs so violently that it hurt worse than the kick.

“Second Form. Rising Scorching Sun!”

The upward slash tore through Akaza's guard. Two fingers fell into the grass. Akaza stumbled, blood spraying from his hand, then drove his other palm down against the blade.

Their faces were inches apart.

“Is this helping?” he rasped.

Rengoku shoved him away.

“You do not get to ask me that!”

Akaza's severed fingers had not returned.

They both looked at his hand.

Then the bridge snapped.

Not all at once. A sharp wooden report. A rope whipping free of the post. The lantern swung wildly, and the carved horse vanished over the edge.

Toma screamed.

Rengoku turned.

The shock of their exchange had split the stone around the bridge anchor. One side of the walkway dropped. Planks tipped, scattering into the gorge, and Toma slid until his outstretched hand caught the remaining rope.

His bundle fell open.

Small wooden shapes spun down towards the white water.

Rengoku was already running.

Behind him, Akaza said something. He did not hear it.

He leaped onto the tilted bridge. The first plank held. The second broke under his heel. He caught the rope with his left hand and dropped, boots skidding down the slanted boards, until he could reach Toma's wrist.

“I have you!”

Toma stared past him.

“The cart—”

“Look at me!”

The man looked.

The remaining anchor began to pull out of the earth.

Rengoku felt the whole bridge move.

He could jump. From the upper boards, alone, perhaps he could reach the bank.

With Toma's weight beneath him, he could not.

He tightened his grip.

Above them, something struck the ground with a deep, heavy thud.

The rope stopped moving.

Akaza knelt at the broken anchor, both arms wrapped around the thick cable. His wounded hand slipped. He caught it again, teeth bared, and braced one bare foot against a half-buried rock.

“Climb,” he said.

Rengoku looked up at him.

“Now!”

He hauled Toma onto a crossbeam. Wood groaned beneath them.

“Hands around my shoulders,” he told him. “Tightly.”

Toma obeyed. His breath came in small, tearing sounds beside Rengoku's ear.

Akaza's foot slid through the dirt.

Rengoku climbed.

Each movement dragged the rope through Akaza's injured hand. Blood darkened the fibres. The rock at his foot shifted, slowly, dragging up a fringe of roots.

The bank was still six arm-lengths away.

Then five.

The river seemed to rise beneath them.

“My brother,” Toma whispered. “I was taking them to my brother.”

“Tell me about him when we are across.”

Four.

Akaza's forearms shook.

“He was little when I left.”

Three.

“Then there is much to catch up on!”

The rock tore free.

Akaza dropped onto his side, dragging the rope across his back and hooking his elbow around the root of a cedar. His shoulder made a sound Rengoku knew too well.

The bridge lurched.

Rengoku jumped.

His free hand caught the bank. Toma's knee struck the edge, slipped, found earth. Rengoku shoved him upward with everything he had.

Toma rolled into the grass.

A hand closed around Rengoku's wrist.

Akaza's uninjured hand.

For a moment, Rengoku could see the strength leaving it.

Then Akaza pulled.

They struck the ground together as the bridge fell away behind them.

The sound went on for a long time.

Rengoku lay on his back, staring at the first stars. His ribs burned. Beside him, Akaza made a small, involuntary noise and clamped his mouth shut.

Across the gorge, Nao stood on the veranda.

Even at that distance, her expression was unmistakable.

Rengoku sat up.

Toma was crying over a little wooden bird. It had caught in the fold of Rengoku's haori, its unfinished wing pressed against a scorch mark.

“One,” Toma said. “I have one.”

Rengoku bowed his head.

“I am sorry. You were crossing safely until we fought.”

Akaza pushed himself upright with his good arm.

“Until we fought,” he repeated.

There was no request in it. No excuse.

Rengoku looked at the ruined bridge. Then at the injured hand resting uselessly in the grass.

He took a clean strip of cloth from inside his uniform and held it out.

“Your hand.”

Akaza stared at it.

“It will hurt less if you stop bleeding on everything,” Rengoku said.

After a moment, Akaza accepted the cloth.
