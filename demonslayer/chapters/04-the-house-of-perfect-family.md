# The House of Perfect Family

The path disappeared when Rengoku looked directly at it.

He stopped with one boot suspended over a root.

“A little to the right,” Akaza said.

Rengoku moved his foot. The ground received it.

“Not quite that far.”

He moved it back.

Behind them, the inn's light had vanished. There were only cedars, black trunks rising through a mist that refused the lantern. Akaza walked ahead with his injured arm held close. Every few steps, he tilted his head as though listening to a sound through a wall.

“What are you following?” Rengoku asked.

“The place I would put a trap.”

Rengoku considered this.

“An unsettling qualification.”

“You asked.”

A white thread slid across the path at throat height.

Akaza stopped. Rengoku lowered the lantern until its light caught a second strand beside his knee, then a third stretching between the first two. Something small hung from the lowest thread.

A wooden name tablet. Its surface had been scraped so often that the grain was almost gone.

SISTER.

The word was written in fresh black ink.

“Do not touch it,” Akaza said.

“I am beginning to understand the general recommendation.”

A house appeared beyond the threads.

It was beautiful. That was the first wrong thing about it.

The veranda had been swept. Paper lanterns glowed beneath the eaves. Through the open doors, Rengoku could see a low table laid for six. Each bowl was exactly the same distance from the next. The surrounding forest had no birds, no insects, no sound but the faint click of something being adjusted.

A woman stood in the doorway.

She wore a faded indigo kimono, with a small square of a different blue sewn into one sleeve. Her hands were folded. A pale thread circled each wrist.

“Welcome home, Father,” she said.

From somewhere inside the house, another voice repeated the sentence with precisely the same cheerful rise.

Rengoku stopped at the first step.

“Good evening. My name is Rengoku. What is yours?”

The woman's fingers pressed together.

“Welcome home, Father.”

Her mouth smiled. Her eyes moved towards the trees behind him.

Akaza glanced at the space above the doorway.

“There.”

Rengoku lifted the lantern.

A thread ran from the woman's wrist through a tiny hole in the lintel. It disappeared into the dark above the ceiling.

“I see it.”

“Not that.”

Beyond it hung a second tablet.

FATHER.

The thread attached to it was slowly uncoiling towards Rengoku's hand.

He stepped aside.

Inside the house, someone sighed.

“You are making her nervous.”

Rui sat at the far end of the table.

White hair fell across one side of his face. Red marks stood out on his pale skin. He held a length of thread between thumb and forefinger, winding it around an empty finger as though measuring a ring.

Beside him sat an older man in a brown work coat. The man's hands rested on the table, palms down. His left thumb had an old carpenter's scar.

“I have somewhere for you,” Rui told Rengoku. “You will be good at it.”

“At what?”

“Taking care of everyone.”

The answer found its mark before Rengoku could guard against it.

He saw the empty place at the table. A pair of sturdy sandals beneath it. Behind the seat, a rack waited for a sword.

“Nobody needs to be frightened here,” Rui said. “As long as we do things properly.”

The woman in the doorway drew a sharp breath.

A red bead appeared beneath the thread around her wrist.

Rengoku's hand moved to his sword.

“Loosen that.”

Rui looked at the woman.

“You are upsetting him.”

Her smile widened. Blood ran over her knuckles.

Rengoku drew.

Akaza's voice cut across the room.

“Wait.”

The blade stopped a hair's breadth from the thread.

The woman was staring at Rengoku's sword with a terror greater than anything he had seen in her face before.

He looked up.

The strand at her wrist continued across the doorway, through a slit in the paper wall, and down to the older man's throat.

Rengoku became very still.

“If you cut the loose end,” Akaza said quietly, “the other end takes the weight.”

The older man swallowed. A thin red line opened above his collar.

Rui tilted his head.

“He understands.”

Akaza's expression hardened.

“I understand what you have made.”

“Then come inside.”

Rengoku sheathed his sword. It took an effort not to do it angrily.

He placed the lantern on the floor just outside the doorway, where its light could reach the veranda. The thread around its handle continued into the house. He did not pull it.

“We will come in,” he said. “She sits down first. With her hands free enough to hold a cup.”

Rui looked from him to the woman.

The thread loosened.

She kept smiling until she had knelt beside the table. Then her face folded with exhaustion.

***

The bowls were empty.

So was the rice pot. Nevertheless, the older man lifted his chopsticks and thanked someone for the meal.

“A very good meal,” the woman said.

Rengoku looked at the white ceramic before him.

“I brought food.”

He lifted Nao's parcel.

Rui's finger twitched.

Every bowl turned an inch towards him.

“That is not what we have for supper.”

Rengoku set the parcel beside his knee.

“Then I will save it.”

Across from him, Akaza lowered himself slowly onto the mat. His shoulder had stiffened during the walk. He braced his good hand against the floor, and the table trembled with the effort he made not to show it.

Rui watched.

“I know you.”

Akaza said nothing.

“You were important.”

“I was dangerous.”

Rui smiled faintly.

“You could be my brother.”

The older man's chopsticks stopped halfway to his mouth.

“Strong enough to keep everyone safe,” Rui continued. “You would not have to leave. Neither of you would.”

Akaza looked towards the open doorway.

Rengoku watched his face and could not tell whether what passed over it was recognition or temptation.

“There are people I would like to see,” Akaza said.

“They could come here.”

“They might not want to.”

Rui's smile disappeared.

The ceiling clicked.

Rengoku counted the strands crossing the room. Too many. Some bright and thin, some almost invisible. The woman's wrists led to the older man's throat. A thread from the older man's sleeve ran beneath the table, through a row of little wooden tablets, and towards the lantern outside.

Their names were not written on the tablets.

Only the places they were supposed to occupy.

“How long have you been here?” Rengoku asked the woman.

She glanced at Rui.

“Since I came home.”

“What did you do before that?”

Her thumb moved over the patch on her sleeve.

The room tightened around them.

Rengoku felt it in the air before he heard the wood creak.

“She is my sister,” Rui said.

“She has heard you.”

“Then why are you asking her?”

The woman looked down at her sleeve.

“I made things,” she whispered.

Rui's chair scraped backwards.

Akaza moved at the same instant, catching a thread above the older man's shoulder between his bandaged palm and the edge of the table.

Blood spread through the cloth.

“Let her finish,” he said.

The pressure eased by a fraction.

“Clothes,” the woman said. “I mended clothes.”

Her breathing quickened.

“My name is Mio.”

One of the wooden tablets split down the centre.

Rui stood.

“You are tired.”

The walls slid shut.

Rengoku rose, catching the edge of the closing door with his foot. The older man lurched upright, choking as the thread at his neck drew taut.

“Akaza!”

“I have it.”

Akaza seized the strand on both sides of the man's throat and spread his hands. The wounded one shook violently.

Rengoku slid a sheathed length of sword between door and frame. Wood cracked.

“Mio. Can you stand?”

She nodded.

“Then come this way.”

She stood.

The house grew another room between them and the doorway.

Rengoku stared at the sudden stretch of floor.

Beyond it stood a second table. Six empty bowls. Six identical places.

And another doorway beyond that.

Rui's voice came from all the walls at once.

“People who want to leave have not been cared for properly.”

The floor beneath Mio snapped upwards.

Rengoku caught her shoulder and pulled her clear. A fan of threads cut through the space her face had occupied, slicing paper and wood into falling strips.

One caught Rengoku across the upper arm.

Blood ran warm inside his sleeve.

He heard Akaza swear. A beam shattered. The older man fell coughing onto his knees.

“Behind me,” Rengoku told Mio.

He drew his sword again.

On the other side of the new room, Akaza stood over the carpenter, one shoulder lower than the other, both hands wet with blood.

Between them, Rui lowered his fingers.

Threads filled the doorway like the ribs of a closing cage.

“We can start supper again,” he said.
