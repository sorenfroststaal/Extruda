# A Name He Cannot Say

Nao reached them by a path that had not existed before.

It came around the bend of the cliff: a narrow shelf of stone, safely fenced, with steps leading up from behind a cedar. She carried a basket, a lantern and an expression that suggested the basket might contain a lecture.

Rengoku stood too quickly.

His ribs objected. He sat down again.

“There is another crossing?” he asked.

“There is one now.”

“Why was it not there before?”

“You were both rather busy deciding there was only one way forward.”

Akaza looked away.

Nao knelt beside Toma first. She examined his wrists, wrapped a blanket around him and asked whether he preferred water or tea.

Nobody asked Rengoku which he preferred.

He accepted this as fair.

By the time they reached the inn, the sky had darkened to a deep, clear blue. Toma went upstairs carrying his bird in both hands. He would not surrender it even while Nao checked his scraped palms.

Akaza stopped at the threshold.

The wisteria hung inches from his face.

Rengoku noticed him looking at it.

“It will not burn you.”

“I know.”

“Then why are you standing outside?”

Akaza glanced past him at the bowls on the table, the folded bedding, the ordinary little room.

“I was waiting to be asked.”

Rengoku stepped aside.

“Come in. Sit there.”

He pointed to the opposite side of the table, leaving plenty of room between them.

Nao examined Akaza's hand. The missing fingers were slowly returning, translucent at the edges, but the torn flesh around them remained swollen and raw.

“I thought—” Rengoku began.

“That he would heal?” Nao asked. “He is. Slowly.”

“I used to—” Akaza stopped.

“I expect you used to do many things.” She set his hand on a folded towel. “Hold still.”

He did.

Rengoku drank his tea. It tasted slightly bitter. He suspected something medicinal and decided not to enquire.

Beyond the open door, there was no sound of footsteps on the bridge.

He had never noticed how often he listened for them.

“The river,” he said. “What would have happened to Toma?”

Nao finished tying the bandage before she answered.

“It carries things away. Names. The reason you were travelling. The face you were trying to remember.”

Akaza's good hand tightened around his cup.

“Can they be found?” Rengoku asked.

“Sometimes. The road is large.”

There was no comfort hidden in the answer.

Rengoku set down his tea.

“Then we will repair the bridge.”

“Yes,” Nao said. “You will.”

She left them with a second pot and went upstairs.

For several breaths, neither man moved.

“You said Muzan was gone,” Rengoku said at last.

Akaza nodded.

“Tell me what you know. Only what you know.”

So he did.

He spoke of an impossible fortress and a boy with a scar on his forehead. Of water moving too quickly for the eye. Of a sword that had reached him without the battle spirit he had trusted for longer than he cared to remember.

He said Tanjiro's name carefully.

Rengoku listened with his hands resting flat on his knees.

“He fought you?”

“With the Water Hashira.”

“And you lost.”

Akaza looked into his cup.

“Yes.”

The answer held more than Rengoku understood. He waited.

“I remembered things,” Akaza said. “People. What I had done before I became a demon. What I had wanted.”

Outside, a loose rope tapped against the empty bridge post.

“I could have kept fighting. My body was trying to. But I remembered her.”

“Who?”

Akaza opened his mouth.

Nothing came out.

Rengoku felt an impatient question rise and kept it behind his teeth.

At last, Akaza said, “Koyuki.”

It was the first name he had spoken without looking at Rengoku.

“She came for me. So did my teacher. My father.”

“Where are they now?”

“There was a door.”

Rengoku glanced towards the stairs.

“And?”

“I could not stay.”

“Could not, or would not?”

The question sounded harsher aloud. Rengoku let it stand.

Akaza stared at his bandage.

“There were people beyond it who loved me. I knew what I had done. I did not know how those things could be in the same room.”

Rengoku had no answer ready for that.

It annoyed him. He had been angry enough to expect all the answers would be easy.

“What did they call you?”

Akaza's shoulders stiffened.

“Before,” Rengoku said. “Your name.”

“Hakuji.”

Rengoku repeated it silently.

The man across the table did not look different. The blue marks remained. So did the blood on his vest. Rengoku could still feel the kick under his ribs and another wound beneath that, one this body no longer carried.

A name did not remove any of it.

“Which should I use?” he asked.

Akaza looked up sharply.

“I do not know.”

“Then tell me when you do.”

Footsteps sounded overhead.

Toma stood at the foot of the stairs with his blanket around his shoulders.

“I cannot sleep.”

Rengoku shifted to make space beside him. Toma sat, placing the wooden bird on the table.

“I remembered my brother's face,” he said. “While I was hanging there. I thought I had lost it.”

“What does he look like?” Rengoku asked.

“Like me. But cross about it.”

Rengoku laughed before he could stop himself.

Toma smiled faintly.

He turned the bird over. A shallow groove marked the place where the missing wing should have joined the body.

“I kept making things to bring him. I thought if there were enough, I would know what to say.”

“What do you want to say?”

Toma rubbed a thumb over the unfinished wood.

“That I missed him.”

The room grew very quiet.

Then a door opened at the back of the inn.

It had been a cupboard when Rengoku came in.

On the other side stood a man with Toma's face and a much less patient expression.

“You could have started with that,” he said.

Toma made a broken little sound. He stood, nearly tripped over the blanket, recovered, and crossed the room.

His brother caught him with both arms.

For a moment, the bird remained on the table.

Then Toma came back for it.

“He likes it,” he said, astonished.

The door closed gently behind them.

Akaza had turned his face towards the garden.

Rengoku let him.

Later, they stood beneath the wisteria while Nao measured the broken anchor with a piece of string.

“I can lift the beams,” Akaza said.

“When your shoulder is better,” Rengoku replied.

“It will manage.”

“That was not a question.”

Akaza looked at him. Rengoku looked back.

For the first time, the silence contained something other than the next blow.

A pale line drifted out of the trees.

Rengoku took it for spider silk until it passed through a wisteria blossom without disturbing the petals. It wound once around the lantern handle.

The flame narrowed.

Akaza stepped forward.

“Do not pull it.”

Rengoku stopped with his fingers an inch away.

The thread tightened. Something chimed in the woods, though there was no wind.

“You recognise it?”

“Rui.”

Rengoku knew the name from reports. A mountain. A family of demons. Slayers sent out and too few returning.

“He is here?”

Akaza's gaze followed the thread into the dark.

“Something of him is.”

Beyond the first trees, a lantern appeared. Then another. Between them stood a small figure in white.

“Father?” it called.

The voice was a child's.

Rengoku took a step.

Akaza caught his sleeve, then released it immediately when Rengoku turned.

“Listen again.”

“Father?” the voice repeated.

The same lift. The same pause. Somewhere behind it, a second voice said the word at precisely the same time.

The lanterns went out.

Rengoku lifted his own. Its light reached the first cedar and stopped there, flat as a hand against glass.

Akaza looked between two unlit trees.

“There is a path to the left.”

“I cannot see it.”

“I can.” He sounded unhappy about it. “It feels familiar.”

Rengoku watched him for a long moment.

Then he set the lantern on the step.

“If we enter that forest, we agree on some things first.”

Akaza waited.

“We protect the people inside. We do not use them to reach an opponent. If you recognise a danger, you tell me before it becomes one.”

“And if I say stop?”

Rengoku glanced at the thread. At the sleeve Akaza had released.

“I will listen.”

“You may not like what I say.”

“That has already happened.”

Akaza almost smiled. The expression vanished before it settled.

“One more thing,” Rengoku said. “What happened between us is not settled by this.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

Akaza looked at the place where the bridge had been.

“You were trying to save that man. I made you turn around.”

Rengoku's grip tightened on the lantern.

“I drew my sword,” he said. “That part was mine.”

Nao stepped between them carrying a cloth-wrapped parcel.

“You can finish considering your many faults on the road. Take these.”

Rengoku looked inside.

Rice balls.

“An excellent provision!”

“Six,” she said. “That means three each.”

He looked mildly wounded.

Akaza looked, briefly, less so.

Rengoku turned towards the trees. Beside him, the man who had killed him stood with one hand bandaged and the other held carefully away from his side.

There was a door upstairs. There would be a bridge to rebuild. Neither had vanished because a child was calling in the dark.

He would come back.

“Show me the path,” he said.

Akaza stepped forward, then stopped at the edge of the light.

“Here. You need to put your foot here.”

Rengoku did.

The forest opened.

Somewhere deep inside it, someone began counting the members of their family.

They stopped when they reached two.
