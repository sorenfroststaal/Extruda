# The Man Beneath the Wisteria

The first thing the old woman said was that one of her sandals had fallen into the river.

She stood halfway across the bridge, gripping both ropes, with one bare foot lifted indignantly above the planks. Wind worried at her sleeves. Far below, the river struck the black sides of the gorge with a sound like trees being torn out by their roots.

Kyojuro Rengoku set his bowl on the inn's veranda.

“A serious setback!” he called. “Please stay exactly where you are!”

“I was not planning to jump.”

“Excellent!”

He crossed the bridge without running. Running made the planks bounce, and frightened people rarely appreciated discovering that the ground could also be frightened. A few violet petals travelled past him, wheeled in the rising air and vanished into the gorge.

The woman watched his feet.

“Do you work here?”

“I help where I can.”

“That is what my husband said whenever he had broken something.”

Rengoku smiled. When he reached her, he crouched and removed one of his boots.

She stared.

“That will never fit.”

“No. But it will keep the splinters out.”

He loosened the fastening of his boot, helped her slip her bare foot inside it and offered his arm. She accepted it with the air of someone allowing a useful piece of furniture to exist.

Together, they crossed.

Rengoku did not look down until she was safely on the bank. A pale scrap turned between the rocks far below. Perhaps her sandal. Perhaps something else.

Things carried into that river did not always remain the things they had been.

At the inn, the woman settled beside the charcoal brazier. Its keeper brought tea and a pair of soft slippers without asking for payment. Her name was Nao. She was small, grey-haired, and had the patience of a stone that had outlasted several troublesome buildings.

“Your rice has gone cold,” she told Rengoku.

“Still delicious!”

“You have not tasted it.”

“I have great confidence in it.”

The old woman laughed into her cup.

Outside, the bridge creaked. Rengoku listened until it settled.

Evening lingered above the Lantern Road: a long amber glow, occasionally deepening into blue, with no reliable connection to how long anyone had slept. The wisteria beside the inn was always flowering. Petals gathered on the steps, yet its branches never grew bare.

Rengoku had stopped trying to count the days.

He had not stopped noticing arrivals.

The bridge brought travellers from the forest to Nao's inn. It was a crossing on the way through the afterlife, not the end of the journey. Beyond the inn, each person had a different road to find.

Rengoku had begun by helping people over the planks. Then he had learned to stay beside them afterwards, listening when they could not explain where they needed to go. He could light the crossing and offer a steady arm. He could not choose their destination for them.

Some travellers knew at once where they were. Others asked for directions to a village, a battlefield, a room where someone was still waiting. Nao gave them tea. Rengoku listened. Sometimes a path appeared among the trees while they spoke, and the traveller stood up before either of them had pointed it out.

He liked those departures.

He always watched until the last footstep faded.

The old woman finished her tea. From her sleeve she took a small wooden comb, missing three teeth.

“I was meant to bring this back,” she said.

“To whom?” Rengoku asked.

“My sister. I borrowed it for my wedding.” She rubbed its worn edge with her thumb. “We argued afterwards. About something important, I imagine. I cannot remember what.”

Behind the wisteria, a narrow opening brightened in the trees.

The woman looked at it. Her face changed so suddenly that Rengoku felt as though he had intruded on a private conversation.

“Oh,” she said. “She has kept the ugly house.”

She stood, leaving Rengoku's borrowed boot neatly beside the slippers.

At the path, she turned back.

“You should eat before helping the next one.”

“I will try!”

“A suspicious answer.”

Then she was gone.

Rengoku remained beneath the flowers a little longer.

There was a door upstairs for him. Plain cedar, a warm strip of light beneath it. He knew who waited on the other side. The first time he had reached for the handle, someone had cried out on the bridge.

The second time, a traveller had arrived unable to remember his own name.

After that, there had always been someone.

Nao came out carrying his bowl.

“You can visit her,” she said.

“I know.”

“The road will still be here afterwards.”

He took the bowl. The rice was warm again. Nao refused to explain this, just as she refused to explain how the inn had exactly enough rooms.

“A remarkable kitchen!” he said.

“A remarkable habit of changing the subject.”

He laughed, though less loudly than before.

Across the gorge, the trees moved.

Not with the wind. Their branches bent away from the road, opening a space where no space had been.

Rengoku put down the bowl.

A young man emerged from the trees in a torn brown coat. He held a cloth bundle against his chest. Pale wood shavings clung to his cuffs.

He approached the bridge, looked down into the gorge and stopped.

Rengoku went to the near end, where the traveller could see him clearly.

“My name is Kyojuro Rengoku! The bridge leads to the inn. There is somewhere to sit, and someone who will listen. What is your name?”

“Toma.” The young man raised his voice above the river. “I am looking for my younger brother.”

“Has he crossed here?”

“I don't know. He died before I did.” Toma looked back at the empty forest. “I thought I would know where to find him when my turn came.”

He loosened the cloth just enough to reveal a little wooden horse.

“I worked with wood. He used to sit beside me and ask what I was making.” His thumb rested on the horse's mane. “I brought him some things.”

Rengoku did not tell him that his brother would be at the inn. He could not promise that.

“Come across, Toma. We can help you look for your next road.”

Toma stepped onto the bridge. With the bundle held in both arms, he had no hand free for the ropes. The planks shifted under him, and he froze.

“One step at a time,” Rengoku said. “I will come and carry the bundle, so you can hold on.”

Rengoku was reaching for his lantern when another figure stepped onto the far bank.

Pink hair. Pale skin crossed by blue lines. A sleeveless red vest.

The distance between them became the length of a railway carriage.

Rengoku heard a boy crying. Felt cold earth against his knees. Remembered the terrible effort of keeping his hands closed around an arm that had gone through his body.

Then the river returned, loud and close.

His fingers had closed around his sword instead of the lantern.

The figure on the opposite bank stopped.

For a moment, neither spoke.

Akaza looked different without a smile. Rengoku remembered delight in those eyes: the pleasure of a worthy opponent, the eagerness of an invitation that made a mockery of everything he protected.

Now there was recognition.

And something that almost resembled dread.

“Kyojuro.”

The name crossed the gorge clearly.

Rengoku drew one inch of steel.

“Stay where you are.”

The man in brown pressed himself against a bridge post. His bundle slipped. Wooden toys scattered at his feet: a horse, a little cart, a bird with one unfinished wing.

Akaza glanced towards the sound.

“I did not follow him.”

“Then you can wait while he crosses.”

Akaza stepped back.

It should have helped.

Instead, Rengoku found himself listening for the movement that would come next. The burst of speed. The fist. The delighted voice asking him, again, to abandon everything that made him human.

Behind him, Nao quietly moved his abandoned bowl indoors.

The traveller crouched to gather his toys, hands shaking so badly that he dropped the horse twice.

Rengoku took the lantern from its hook.

Its flame leaned sharply towards the far bank.

He had carried it through rain that fell upwards, through mist thick enough to hide his own hands. It had never burned his fingers.

Now the handle was hot.

“You,” he said, looking directly at the traveller. “Keep your eyes on me. We are going to get you across.”

The man nodded once.

Rengoku stepped onto the bridge.

At the far end, Akaza lifted his head.

“There is something I need to tell you.”

“There are several things I need to ask.”

Rengoku's voice was perfectly steady.

That frightened him more than if it had shaken.
